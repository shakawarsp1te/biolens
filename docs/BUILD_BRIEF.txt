# BioLens — Claude Code Master Build Brief

## READ THIS FIRST

You are building **BioLens**, an investor-oriented biotechnology intelligence application.

Do not treat this as a generic AI chatbot.

Do not treat this as a stock-picking application.

Do not attempt to index the entire biomedical literature.

Do not build the web application yet.

The first product is:

# **An iOS-first mobile application**

Build it using a cross-platform framework so Android can eventually follow, but optimize V1 exclusively around the iPhone experience.

The initial product should be small enough to build, test, and release to beta users within approximately 30 days.

The long-term ambition can be large.

The MVP cannot be.

---

# 1. PRODUCT VISION

## One-sentence vision

**BioLens helps investors discover the frontier of biotechnology and understand the science behind emerging companies, therapies, and clinical-trial catalysts.**

Alternative positioning:

> **The frontier of biotech, explained.**

Alternative:

> **Discover emerging biotech. Understand the science.**

Alternative:

> **From molecule to market.**

The application should feel like a combination of:

* biotech intelligence terminal
* scientific translator
* emerging-company discovery platform
* clinical-trial interpreter
* investor education product

But it should have the simplicity of a consumer application.

The user should not feel like they are using Bloomberg.

The user should feel like:

> “BioLens just made something that would have taken me an hour of research understandable in three minutes.”

---

# 2. THE FUNDAMENTAL PROBLEM

Biotechnology investing has an unusually high knowledge barrier.

An investor may encounter:

> “Company X reports Phase II data showing a 31% ORR, median DOR not reached, and median PFS of 8.4 months in second-line biomarker-positive NSCLC.”

That sentence requires understanding:

* cancer biology
* clinical-trial design
* response criteria
* statistics
* standard of care
* drug mechanisms
* biomarkers
* competitive treatments
* regulatory development
* commercial markets
* company pipelines

The information may be distributed among:

* ClinicalTrials.gov
* company investor relations pages
* SEC filings
* scientific publications
* FDA materials
* conference abstracts
* drug labels
* press releases

BioLens should transform these fragmented sources into:

### What happened?

### What does the science mean?

### How strong is the evidence?

### What is still unknown?

### Who competes with this company?

### Why might investors care?

### What happens next?

# 3. BIOlens IS NOT A STOCK PICKER

This is important.

Never design BioLens around:

**BUY**

**SELL**

**STRONG BUY**

**UNDERVALUED**

**PRICE TARGET**

Do not make personalized investment recommendations.

BioLens is an:

> **investment research, scientific interpretation, and company-discovery tool.**

The application can explain investor implications.

For example:

> “This result reduces one major clinical uncertainty surrounding the program.”

Good.

> “The stock should rise 30%.”

Bad.

It can say:

> “This asset represents a substantial portion of the company's clinical pipeline, making the result strategically important.”

Good.

It should not say:

> “You should buy the stock before Phase III.”

Bad.

# 4. THE CENTRAL PRODUCT LOOP

Every BioLens experience should revolve around:

# DISCOVER

Find an interesting biotech company or event.

↓

# UNDERSTAND

Understand the biology and clinical evidence.

↓

# EVALUATE

Understand strengths, weaknesses, uncertainty, and competition.

↓

# FOLLOW

↓

# RETURN

Come back when new data appear.

This creates repeat usage.

# 5. INITIAL TARGET USER

Do not build initially for professional pharmaceutical scientists.

Do not build initially for hedge-fund analysts with access to expensive institutional terminals.

The initial target user is:

## The intelligent biotech-curious investor who does not have years of scientific training.

Examples:

* college students interested in biotech investing
* finance students researching biotech
* retail investors interested in biotechnology
* young biotech professionals
* consulting students
* VC interns
* healthcare-investment-club members
* scientists interested in the commercial side of biotechnology

Knowledge level:

They may understand:

* market cap
* revenue
* clinical trial phases

But they may not understand:

* hazard ratios
* RECIST
* PFS versus OS
* biomarkers
* target biology
* drug modality differences
* statistical multiplicity
* dose-response relationships

BioLens bridges that gap.

# 12. “EMERGING” MUST MEAN SOMETHING

Do not make “emerging biotech” a random editorial label.

* it is publicly traded
* it remains primarily biotechnology-focused
* it has at least one clinical-stage therapeutic program
* it does not have a diversified large-pharma commercial portfolio
* a significant portion of company value still depends on pipeline execution

Market capitalization can eventually be incorporated, but do not make real-time market-cap data a dependency for V1.

A company can be marked:

### Scaling

### Established

# 26. STATISTICAL ENGINE — ABSOLUTE RULE

The application must never reduce clinical-trial analysis to:

> p < 0.05 = success.

Implement a structured statistics interpretation layer.

Statistics must be treated as one component of evidence.

---

Classify endpoints.

## Time-to-event

Examples:

* overall survival
* progression-free survival
* event-free survival

Important quantities:

median survival

hazard ratio

95% confidence interval

p-value

follow-up

number of events

---

## Binary

Examples:

* objective response rate
* complete response
* remission rate

Important quantities:

responders

denominator

percentage

confidence interval

control percentage

absolute difference

---

## Continuous

Examples:

* change in disease score
* biomarker level
* symptom score

Important quantities:

baseline

change

between-group difference

confidence interval

statistical test

---

# 28. PRIMARY VS SECONDARY ENDPOINTS

Always identify:

### Primary endpoint

### Key secondary endpoints

### Exploratory endpoints

Do not treat them equally.

The primary endpoint should carry the greatest inferential weight.

If the primary endpoint fails:

Do not portray positive secondary endpoints as equivalent to a successful trial unless the statistical testing plan supports that interpretation.

Example:

> “The primary endpoint was not met. Positive findings in secondary outcomes may still be scientifically interesting but should be interpreted cautiously.”

---

# 29. MULTIPLICITY

Multiple testing increases false-positive risk.

Therefore:

If several endpoints are tested:

attempt to determine whether the company reported:

* hierarchical testing
* alpha allocation
* multiplicity adjustment
* prespecified testing sequence

If unclear:

label it.

Example:

> “The release reports nominal significance for this secondary endpoint, but BioLens could not verify whether the analysis was protected by the study's multiplicity-control procedure.”

This is extremely important.

---

# 30. INTERIM ANALYSES

Interim trials may use significance thresholds different from 0.05.

Never automatically judge an interim result by:

`p < 0.05`

Instead:

look for:

* prespecified interim boundary
* alpha spending
* statistical-analysis plan description

If absent:

state:

> “The appropriate interim significance threshold was not available from the reviewed sources.”

---

# 31. CONFIDENCE INTERVALS

Confidence intervals are important.

Do not show a point estimate alone when CI is reported.

Example:

HR:

0.72

95% CI:

0.55–0.94

Explain:

> The estimated treatment effect favors the experimental arm, but the range of plausible effects is reflected by the confidence interval.

Do not imply:

> “There is a 95% probability that the true hazard ratio lies within the interval.”

Avoid that common misinterpretation.

---

# 32. EFFECT SIZE VS STATISTICAL SIGNIFICANCE

Always separate:

## Statistical evidence

from:

## Magnitude of clinical benefit

Example:

A trial may show:

p = 0.01

but:

PFS improvement only 0.8 months.

BioLens should say:

> “The difference is statistically significant as reported, but the absolute clinical benefit appears modest and should be weighed against toxicity, quality of life, and existing treatments.”

Conversely:

A small early trial could show:

large effect

but wide uncertainty.

BioLens should say:

> “The observed response is large, but the small sample size creates substantial uncertainty around the true effect.”

---

# 33. HAZARD RATIO INTERPRETATION

For time-to-event outcomes:

HR < 1 generally favors treatment if the event is undesirable.

But explain carefully.

Example:

HR = 0.70

Use:

> “The treatment group experienced an estimated 30% lower instantaneous hazard of progression or death over the analyzed period.”

Do not say:

> “30% of patients were saved.”

Do not say:

> “Patients lived 30% longer.”

Those are incorrect interpretations.

---

# 34. OBJECTIVE RESPONSE RATE

For ORR:

always retrieve:

responders

evaluable population

confidence interval if available

complete versus partial responses

follow-up

Example:

12 responses / 20 evaluable patients

ORR:

60%

But display:

> **12 of 20 evaluable patients**

because a percentage without denominator can be misleading.

---

# 35. DURATION OF RESPONSE

If ORR is impressive but follow-up is short:

BioLens should flag:

> “The response rate is encouraging, but durability remains uncertain because median follow-up is limited.”

If median DOR is not reached:

Do not interpret this automatically as extraordinary efficacy.

Explain:

> “Median duration of response has not yet been reached, meaning more than half of responding patients remained in response at the analysis cutoff. Longer follow-up is needed.”

---

# 36. SINGLE-ARM TRIALS

This is extremely important.

For a single-arm trial:

Never present efficacy as though the treatment beat a control arm.

Use:

> “This trial does not contain a concurrent control group.”

Historical comparison can be shown only with an explicit warning:

> “Cross-trial comparison is approximate because patient populations, eligibility, assessment timing, and trial methods may differ.”

---

# 37. CROSS-TRIAL COMPARISON

Never simply rank:

Drug A ORR 60%

Drug B ORR 45%

therefore A is better.

Before comparing, show differences in:

* treatment line
* patient biomarkers
* prior therapies
* sample size
* endpoint definitions
* follow-up
* trial phase
* dosage
* combination therapy
* independent versus investigator review

Label comparisons:

### Directionally informative

rather than:

### Head-to-head evidence

unless it genuinely is head-to-head.

---

# 38. EARLY-STAGE DATA

Phase I datasets often have:

small n

multiple doses

selected evaluable populations

short follow-up

frequent updates.

Therefore early data should receive labels such as:

# Early signal

rather than:

# Proven efficacy

---

# 39. STATISTICAL INTERPRETATION CLASSIFICATION

Create four evidence interpretations:

## 1. Confirmatory Positive

Requirements generally include:

* prespecified primary endpoint met
* appropriate statistical framework
* adequate trial design
* relevant uncertainty reported

Do not apply casually.

---

## 2. Encouraging Signal

Typical for:

* early phase
* single-arm trial
* strong biological activity
* limited sample size

---

## 3. Inconclusive

Examples:

* endpoint unclear
* insufficient follow-up
* small cohort
* conflicting results
* missing statistical context

---

## 4. Negative on Primary Endpoint

Primary endpoint not met.

Secondary signals can still be displayed separately.

---

# 40. NEVER INVENT STATISTICS

If a press release provides:

ORR

but no confidence interval,

do not ask the LLM to invent one.

If raw counts are available and a mathematically valid interval can be computed deterministically, the backend may calculate one.

But clearly label:

### BioLens calculated

versus:

### Company reported

Every number needs provenance.

---

# 41. DETERMINISTIC STATISTICS BEFORE LLM

Where possible:

Use Python functions to:

* calculate percentages
* calculate absolute differences
* validate ratios
* parse confidence intervals
* classify sample size
* compare reported thresholds

The LLM should explain the results.

It should not perform critical arithmetic if code can do it.

---

# 42. FACT VS INTERPRETATION

Every BioLens output should internally distinguish:

## FACT

Directly supported by source.

Example:

> Trial enrolled 142 participants.

## CALCULATED

Derived mathematically.

Example:

> 18/30 = 60% response rate.

## INTERPRETATION

Analytical conclusion.

Example:

> Response appears encouraging for an early-stage cohort.

## SPECULATION

Scenario.

Example:

> Positive larger-cohort data could increase partnership interest.

These should never be blurred.

# 44. THE BIG DATA PROBLEM — DO NOT INGEST EVERYTHING

This is a critical architectural requirement.

BioLens does **not** need every biomedical paper in its database.

Do not:

* download all PubMed
* embed millions of abstracts
* store every FDA record
* crawl the entire internet
* index every biotechnology company

That is unnecessary and would destroy the MVP timeline.

Instead implement:

# On-demand retrieval + aggressive caching.

# 45. RETRIEVAL FLOW

Suppose a user analyzes:

`ABC-101 Phase II results`

Step 1:

Extract entities:

company

drug

target

disease

NCT ID if present

Step 2:

Query ClinicalTrials.gov.

Step 3:

Identify canonical trial.

Step 4:

Query PubMed specifically for:

NCT ID

drug name

drug alias

target + indication

Step 5:

Retrieve only the most relevant papers.

Initially:

maximum ~10–20 candidates.

Step 6:

Rerank them.

Highest priority:

exact NCT ID match

exact drug match

trial publication

most relevant disease

recent relevant study

Step 7:

Use only approximately:

3–8 important sources

for the final analysis.

This is enough for the majority of use cases.

---

# 46. DO NOT SUMMARIZE PAPERS THAT DO NOT MATTER

A paper is relevant if it helps answer:

* what is the target?
* why is it biologically important?
* what does the drug do?
* what previous evidence exists?
* what does the current trial show?
* what is the competitive benchmark?

Anything else is likely noise.
